1 — DELIVERY: "did it get through, and if not, why?"

1a — Questions answered. The four things a GM actually asks, each answered in a sentence: Did it leave? Did it arrive? Have they read it? Did they answer? The first "no" is the one that matters, so it carries the colour, the reason and the button that fixes it; everything after it is greyed. Reads like a person explaining it to you.
1b — Postmarks. The diegetic version: each thing that happened to the message is a stamp on it (SENT · VIA CITINET · DELIVERED · OPENED), a missing one is a faint outline, a failure a heavy red stamp. Fun and quick to scan; it explains less on its own, so the reason still sits under it.

2 — GETTING READY: "what will my players run into?"

One card per player showing what their Agent will look like when they open it — a little screen, the way they'll see it — and one sentence underneath saying what that means and what to do. Instead of a checklist of settings, the GM sees the consequence: "Rache will see the address setup screen", "Judy has nobody to write to", "V is good to go". Cards needing you sort first; the top edge is the verdict.

3 — TERMINAL ACCESS: "who can get into this, and how likely are they?"

The account's lock written as a rule in one sentence (with the pieces coloured: network, ways in, what failing costs), then a table of the players' characters: can they even see it, which way in suits them best, and their real odds from their sheet (Interface 14 + d10 vs DV 18 → 70%). The GM learns the lock by reading it, and can tell at a glance whether it's too easy or impossible for this party. Lockouts and who's logged in sit in the same table.
